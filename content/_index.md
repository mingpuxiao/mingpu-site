I am a Ph.D. candidate in Political Science at UC San Diego, in the fields of comparative politics and political methodology, with a specialization in computational social science. My research is in comparative and international political economy and in authoritarian politics, with China as its main case. I study how authoritarian regimes win public support and build state capacity, how citizens contest them, and how state-directed development shapes paths to democratization. I also study how external shocks, such as sanctions and trade disputes, affect domestic politics and economic institutions.

My dissertation asks why state ownership, once a socialist economic institution, has outlived the socialist world that built it. State-owned enterprises anchor the world's second-largest economy, and governments in the United States, Europe, and beyond have taken equity in steel, energy, semiconductors, and critical minerals, often on national-security grounds. I argue that external threat drives the return of state ownership by lending it popular legitimacy, and that leaders use state-controlled resources to build state capacity and stay in power.

My research draws on a multi-method design that combines causal inference with observational data, computational text analysis, and survey experiments.
